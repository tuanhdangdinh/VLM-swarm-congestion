## 1. Garg et al. — "Online Motion Planning for Connected Multi-Robot Systems using Vision Language Models as High-level Planners" (PMLR 2026; VLM-GCBF+)

Source PDF (21 pp. incl. supplementary): [mit-realm.github.io PDF](https://mit-realm.github.io/VLM-gcbfplus-website/files/Online_planning_using_VLMs.pdf). Header: "Proceedings of Machine Learning Research vol vvv:1–21, 2026". Authors: Kunal Garg, Devika Shaj Kumar Nair (ASU), Songyuan Zhang, Jacob Arkin, Chuchu Fan (MIT). arXiv:2404.06413 is the earlier "Foundation Models to the Rescue" version; this PMLR PDF is the current one and was the one read.

### Takeaway
A VLM is queried *reactively* on deadlock detection to output a leader + waypoints as a JSON object; the output is parsed and executed directly with **no repair, feasibility check, or verification of the VLM plan** — safety rests entirely on the provably safe GCBF+ low-level controller. Headline per-model reach-rate numbers exist only as bar/box plots (Fig. 5, Fig. 12), not as numbers in text or tables.

### Cited Findings
- **Models evaluated (VLM mode, Fig. 3 & Fig. 5):** GPT-5, GPT-5-Low (low reasoning effort), GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4, Claude Opus 4.1, Claude Sonnet 3.5, Claude 3 Haiku, Claude 3 Opus, Claude 3 Sonnet. **LLM baselines (text-only):** GPT-5, GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude 3 Opus (Fig. 5 "Opus3"); Fig. 3 also evaluates GPT-4-Turbo for waypoint generation (Sec. 5 notes "with the exception of GPT-4-Turbo" in the accuracy trend). Non-learned baselines: A*-based high-level planner and a "Random" leader+waypoints planner. — [PDF](https://mit-realm.github.io/VLM-gcbfplus-website/files/Online_planning_using_VLMs.pdf) pp. 6-9
- **Deadlock-detection trigger (Sec. 4, p. 4), verbatim:** "the high-level planner is triggered upon detection of a deadlock, which we define as a situation when the average speed of the MRS falls below a minimum threshold δv > 0 ... and the average distance of the agents from their goals is at least δd > 0". Experiments use **δv = 0.2 and δd = 0.4** (Sec. 6.1, p. 8), and after a trigger "the MRS remains in the same leader-follower configuration for T_LF = 100 steps. This helps prevent Zeno behaviors and sets an upper bound on the frequency at which the foundation model is queried." — p. 8
- **Agent counts / environments (Sec. 6.1, pp. 7-8):** "Room" N=5 (20 hand-crafted environments); "Maze" N=25 with M=100 obstacles (20 environments) and N=50 with M=375 (11 environments). Time budgets: "a fixed number of steps T = 3000 for Room environments, T = 4000 for the Maze environment with N = 25, and T = 5000 for the Maze environment with N = 50." — p. 8
- **Metrics (Sec. 6, p. 7):** "i) the reach rate or the percentage of agents reaching their goals; ii) the number of high-level interventions needed during the rollout; iii) the time taken in calling the high-level planner; and iv) the tokens used in each intervention". Fig. 5 reports both "the ratio of the trajectories where *all* the agents reach their goals" (bar) and "the ratio of agents that reach their goals over all agents" (orange dot). — pp. 7, 9
- **Numbers stated in text:** low-level controller alone: "the reach rate for just the low-level controller is exactly 0" (p. 8); random planner: "a randomly chosen leader, along with randomly chosen waypoints, yields a reach rate of less than 0.5 in all cases" (p. 9); VLM token cost: "the average number of total tokens required by image-based VLMs remains similar (≈ 1000) across the various environments" (p. 10); GPT-5 reasoning effort: "higher reasoning requires significantly longer inference time (up to 4 times), as well as higher output tokens (up to 2 times), but without a clear trend in performance improvement" (p. 10). A* comparison: "For the small-scale Room environments, the mean reach rate for A* across 20 test environments is higher than VLMs but is lower than all of the LLMs. For the large-scale Maze environments, the mean reach rate for A* is lower than most of the VLMs." (p. 9). — [PDF](https://mit-realm.github.io/VLM-gcbfplus-website/files/Online_planning_using_VLMs.pdf)
- **Spatial-reasoning pre-study (Sec. 5, pp. 6-7):** 100 randomly generated 10x10 grid environments; scene-interpretation accuracy "is defined as the percentage of correctly identified locations of the obstacles' vertices, the agent, and the goal (within a tolerance of 0.5 units)"; separate feasible-waypoint-generation accuracy; results only in Fig. 3 bars (e.g., GPT-5 ≈85% scene / ≈99% waypoint; Claude 3 Haiku ≈7% scene). Trend quoted: "GPT-5>GPT4o>GPT4o-mini>GPT-4". — pp. 6-7
- **Prompt contents (Sec. 4.1, p. 5 + Appendix C, pp. 16-18):** three components — (1) Task description (fixed offline; includes safety/connectivity requirements, planner role, number of waypoints P); (2) Environment state: "the environment state is constructed via a base64 encoded JPEG image that includes the location of the agents, their goals, and the obstacles seen by the MRS so far ... whose information comes from the LiDAR data", plus "we provide their text description as well" for agent/goal locations; (3) Desired output: "we request the generated response to be formatted as a JSON object with an expected output of the form {"Leader" : Id, "Waypoints" : [[x1, y1], . . . , [xP , yP ]]}" (Sec. 4.1-4.2, pp. 5-6). Full prompt screenshots in Figs. 6-9; example output (App. C.3, p. 17): Claude3-Opus returns {"Leader": 1, "Waypoint": [[2.8, 4.5], [5.5, 4.5], [5.5, 7.9]]}.
- **Repair/verification of VLM output: none found anywhere in the 21 pages.** The architectural stance is explicit (Sec. 1, p. 2): "we are instead interested in using them *reactively* to resolve a class of failure modes in low-level controllers for MRS, namely deadlocks. This helps to ensure that the VLM does not lead to a violation of safety as it is taken care of by the provably safe low-level control policy." No JSON-retry, waypoint feasibility check, plan repair, or output validation step is described; the only downstream mechanism is the GCBF+ controller (Appendix D) which treats the waypoint as the leader's temporary goal. — pp. 2, 5-6, 17-19
- **Ablation insight relevant to plan-quality provenance (App. E.1, p. 21), verbatim:** "the number of high-level planner interventions is generally inversely proportional to the quality of the plan they suggest. As evident from the figure, the number of interventions with partial information is, on average, more than the case when all the known information is provided ... We infer that the quality of the plan provided drops as the amount of data provided to the foundation models decreases." (i.e., intervention count is used as an *indirect* proxy for plan quality — no direct plan-validity metric exists). — p. 21
- **Conclusions/future work (Sec. 7, p. 10), verbatim highlights:** "The high performance of the zero-shot foundation models, particularly newer and larger models such as GPT-5 with low reasoning effort, across various MRS environments is good evidence that they are promising high-level planners for online motion planning problems. Recently, there has been an increasing interest in automatic prompt optimization for black-box foundation models ... Future work includes applying such techniques to the problem of deadlock resolution to maximize the performance of VLMs as a high-level planner." (No limitations section beyond this; no mention of output verification as future work.) — p. 10

### Inferences
- For the thesis argument: this is a clean example of "trust the VLM plan, let the safe controller absorb errors" — the paper never audits how often VLM waypoints were bad; badness only shows up indirectly as more interventions/lower reach rate. A per-leg plan-provenance metric has no counterpart here.
- Exact reach-rate values per model cannot be cited numerically; cite the figure (Fig. 5) or the qualitative text statements above.

### Gaps
- No numeric per-model reach-rate table exists in the paper (box/bar plots only), so exact numbers like "GPT-4o reach rate = X" cannot be quoted.
- The OpenReview page (vz1p8TRRAb) was not separately fetched; the website PDF was treated as authoritative.

## 2. LLMDR — "LLM-Driven Deadlock Detection and Resolution in Multi-Agent Pathfinding" (arXiv:2503.00717)

Source: [arXiv:2503.00717v1 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.00717) (7 pp.; Seungbae Seo, Junghwan Kim, Minjeong Shin, Bongwon Suh, Seoul National Univ.; "submitted to the IEEE").

### Takeaway
The LLM's entire output is a JSON classification — deadlocked-agent groups plus a strategy label ("leader" or "radiation") per group — which is converted into priorities fed to PIBT; PIBT (not the LLM) generates the collision-free 1-step actions, so plan validity is guaranteed by construction and the paper reports **no plan-validity or intervention-rate statistics for the LLM itself**.

### Cited Findings
- **Models:** gpt-3.5-turbo and gpt-4o as the LLM (Sec. V.A: "We tested with gpt-3.5-turbo and gpt-4o as the underlying language models"); base learned MAPF models: PRIMAL, DHC, DCC, SACHA, EPH* ("EPH* denotes the base model of EPH without its original inference strategies and ensemble", Table I caption). — [arXiv:2503.00717](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00717)
- **Benchmarks/maps (Sec. V.A):** four standard MAPF benchmark maps: random32 (32x32), random64 (64x64), den312d (65x81 game map), warehouse (161x63); 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 agents; max timestep 256 (random32/random64/den312d) or 512 (warehouse); 300 test instances in experiment 1, 100 in experiments 2-3. Hyperparameters: detection window length 4, execution plan length 16.
- **What the LLM outputs (Sec. IV.A, Prompt 1):** three stages — classify deadlocks ("No movement, Wandering" vs. normal), group deadlocked agents within 2-Manhattan distance, assign per-group strategy; output format verbatim from Prompt 1: `{ "agent_id": [Agent IDs in the same group], "solution": "leader" or "radiation" }`. "Leader" is used "when any agent in the group has a goal more than 8 Manhattan units away"; "radiation" (move away from deadlock center) when all agents are near their goals. Strategies become agent/action priorities inside PIBT (Algorithm 2, StrategizedPIBT), which "is used to derive collision-free 1-step action set". — pp. 3-4
- **Headline numbers vs. baselines (Table I, warehouse map, EL = avg episode length / SR = success rate):** 64 agents: PRIMAL 512.00/0%, DHC 512.00/1% -> DHC+LLMDR 350.21/**83%**; DCC 473.92/14% -> DCC+LLMDR 319.02/**74%**; SACHA 437.29/28%; EPH* 496.22/6% -> EPH*+LLMDR 357.72/**68%**. 32 agents: DHC 432.28/28% -> DHC+LLMDR 258.18/94%; DCC 335.81/58% -> DCC+LLMDR 231.50/93%. — Table I, p. 5
- **LLM-capability comparison (Table II, DCC base):** warehouse 64 agents: DCC 468.48/16%; DCC+LLMDR(gpt-3.5-turbo) 347.96/71%; DCC+LLMDR(gpt-4o) 324.55/**74%**. random64 64 agents: DCC 147.31/77%; gpt-3.5-turbo 138.01/99%; gpt-4o 132.31/94%. Text: "while gpt-3.5-turbo tends to exhibit longer average episode lengths than the base model at similar success rates, this effect is much less noticeable in gpt-4o ... as the language model's reasoning improves, LLMDR better distinguishes deadlocks from non-deadlocks, leading to more precise interventions." — Sec. V.C, p. 5
- **PIBT's role:** PIBT is a *component* of LLMDR (resolution engine), not a compared baseline; no plain-PIBT column exists in any table. — Secs. III-IV
- **Hyperparameter/intervention-granularity analysis (Table III, 64 agents warehouse):** detection-window length 2/4/8 -> SR 71.00/74.00/73.00%; execution plan length 8/16/32 -> SR 70.00/74.00/75.00%, EL 323.66/324.55/339.06; "a length of 16 offers a balance between providing enough intervention steps to resolve deadlocks and minimizing the risk of decreased efficiency". Interpretive text: "increasing the detection window length beyond 4 does not yield significant performance improvements. This suggests that most deadlocks in our scenarios are due to stagnation rather than oscillation or wandering." — Sec. V.D, p. 6
- **Limitation (Sec. VI), verbatim:** "the computational cost of LLMs remains a challenge, limiting LLMDR's use in resource-constrained or real-time scenarios." — p. 6

### Inferences
- LLMDR reports *no* statistic on how often the LLM misclassifies deadlocks, how often its JSON fails to parse, or how often its strategy is overridden — validity is structurally delegated to PIBT. This supports the thesis claim that plan-provenance-style accounting is absent from this literature.

### Gaps
- No per-call LLM accuracy/intervention statistics exist in the paper (only end-task SR/EL), so nothing can be cited for "plan-validity statistics" beyond their absence.

## 3. RoCo — "RoCo: Dialectic Multi-Robot Collaboration with Large Language Models" (arXiv:2307.04738, ICRA 2024)

Source: [arXiv:2307.04738v1 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.04738) (20 pp.; Mandi, Jain, Song, Columbia).

### Takeaway
Yes — RoCo reports **average re-plan attempts as a headline metric**: Table 2 reports, for every method x task, success rate, env steps, and "average number of re-plan attempts used across all runs", over 20 runs per task; a 5-stage plan-validation suite (parsing, task constraints, IK, collision, waypoints) gates execution and feeds failure text back for re-prompting.

### Cited Findings
- **Validation pipeline (Sec. 3.2, p. 3):** a proposed sub-task plan "is first passed through a set of validations before going into execution. If any of the checks fail, the feedback is appended to each agent's prompt and another round of dialog begins." The five checks in order: "1. Text Parsing ... 2. Task Constraints ... 3. IK ... 4. Collision Checking ... 5. Valid Waypoints ... The agents are allowed to re-plan until reaching a maximum number of attempts, after which the current round ends without any execution." — p. 3
- **Evaluation metric definition (Sec. 5.1, p. 5):** "we evaluate the task performance on three metrics: 1) task success rate within the finite rounds; 2) number of environment steps ... 3) average number of re-plan attempts at each round before an environment action is executed — this reflects the agents' ability to understand and use environment feedback to improve their plans." — p. 5
- **Table 2 numbers (success rate ± se; steps; re-plans), 20 runs/task, GPT-4:** Dialog (ours): Pack Grocery 0.44±0.06 / 9.9 / **3.5**; Arrange Cabinet 0.75±0.10 / 4.7 / 2.0; Sweep Floor 0.95±0.05 / 7.1 / 1.0; Make Sandwich 0.80±0.08 / 10.2 / 1.7; Sort Cubes 0.93±0.06 / 4.9 / 1.3; Move Rope 0.65±0.11 / 2.5 / 3.1. Central Plan (oracle): 0.82±0.06 / 11.1 / 3.9; 0.90±0.07 / 4.0 / 2.7; 1.00±0.00 / 8.4 / 2.0; 0.96±0.04 / 8.8 / 1.2; 0.70±0.10 / 8.6 / 2.6; 0.50±0.11 / 2.3 / 3.9. Dialog w/o Feedback is pinned at re-plan = 1.0 by construction (failed plan discarded, "each episode is given twice the budget of episode length"). — Table 2, p. 5
- **Toy 3D path-planning study (Sec. 3.3, p. 4 + App. 13):** "we ask an LLM (GPT-4) to plan multi-agent paths in a 3D grid ... Given feedback on failed plans, allowing up to 5 attempts, GPT-4 obtains 86.7% success rate over 30 runs, using on average 2.73 attempts." App. 13.2: success varies with temperature, best ≈0.6; 10x10x10 grids need more re-planning attempts than 5x5x5. — pp. 4, 17-18
- **No plan-validity *rate* is reported** (e.g., % of first-attempt plans passing validation); the re-plan count is the only provenance-adjacent statistic. Failure analysis is qualitative (App. 12.1: "Factual Errors in Dialog", "Errors in Motion Trajectory Execution"). — pp. 15-16
- **Models:** GPT-4 for all main results (Sec. 5.1); the text-only RoCoBench-Text dataset additionally evaluates GPT-4-0314, GPT-4-0613, GPT-3.5-turbo, Claude-v1 (Table 4, p. 8). Real-world: GPT-4 + OWL-ViT perception, human-robot block sorting, success 9/10 and 8/10 (oracle-corrected human) vs 7/10 and 6/10 (imperfect human) (Table 3, p. 6).
- **Limitations (Sec. 7, p. 8), verbatim headers + key lines:** "Oracle state information in simulation. RoCo assumes perception ... is accurate." / "Open-loop execution. ... LLMs can't recognize or find means to handle such execution-level errors." / "LLM-query Efficiency. We rely on querying pre-trained LLMs for generating every single response in an agent's dialog, which can be cost expensive ... Response delay from LLM querying is not desirable for tasks that are dynamic or speed sensitive." — p. 8

### Inferences
- RoCo is the strongest precedent for treating "how many times did the checker bounce the plan" as a first-class reported number — but it counts *whole-plan rejection iterations until validity*, not the fraction of a plan preserved vs. overridden. Useful contrast for defining per-leg plan provenance.

### Gaps
- Per-check breakdown (how often IK vs. collision vs. parsing failed) is not reported anywhere in the paper.

## 4. Grover, Liu, Sycara — "The Before, During and After of Multi-Robot Deadlock" (IJRR 2023; arXiv:2206.01781)

Source: [arXiv:2206.01781v1 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.01781) (18 pp.).

### Takeaway
Confirmed decentralized: the abstract promises "a provably-correct decentralized algorithm for deadlock resolution". "Deadlock resolution" means a hand-designed three-phase switching controller (CBF-QP -> feedback-linearized rotation that swaps robot positions at exactly the safety distance -> proportional go-to-goal), with proofs of no-relapse and goal convergence — but only for 2 and 3 robots.

### Cited Findings
- **Decentralized, verbatim (Abstract):** "we analyze the number of admissible robot configurations in deadlock and develop a provably-correct decentralized algorithm for deadlock resolution to safely deliver the robots to their goals. This algorithm is validated in simulations as well as experimentally on Khepera-IV robots." — [arXiv:2206.01781](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.01781), p. 1
- **Deadlock definition (Definition 1, Sec. 3, p. 4):** "Robot i is in deadlock if u_i* = 0 and the prescribed nominal control û_i ≠ 0 <=> p_i ≠ p_d_i" — i.e., the CBF-QP outputs zero velocity although the nominal goal controller is nonzero and the robot is away from its goal. ("We do not analyze 'livelock' in this paper", p. 4.)
- **Geometric characterization:** Theorem 3 ("Safety Margin Apart", p. 10): "In deadlock, the two robots are separated by the safety distance and the robots are on the verge of violating safety"; Theorem 5 (p. 11): the system deadlock set "is measure zero"; deadlock = force equilibrium between goal attraction and active-constraint repulsion (KKT/duality interpretation, Sec. 5); number of admissible deadlock configurations grows combinatorially — MATLAB enumeration gives exactly {1, 1, 4, 18} configurations for N = {1,2,3,4} vs. their analytic lower bound {1,1,4,15} (p. 10).
- **Resolution mechanism (Sec. 6, pp. 11-12):** three phases — "Phase 1: CBF-QP ... To detect the incidence of deadlock, we continuously compare ||u*||, ||p − p_d|| against small thresholds. Once these thresholds are small enough, we declare deadlock detected and switch control to Phase 2. [Phase 2] we rotate the robots around each other to swap positions while maintaining the safe distance" (feedback linearization enforcing ||Δp_12|| = D_s during rotation, i.e., the robots slide along the boundary of the safe set), then "Phase 3, which simply uses the prescribed [proportional] controllers." Explicitly contrasted with perturbation methods: "For deadlock resolution, we do not consider additive perturbations since they lack formal guarantees." (p. 3)
- **Guarantees (Theorem 8, p. 12 / Theorem 9, p. 17):** "The three phase strategy ensures that the robots will not fall back in deadlock and will converge to their goals." Proof shows inter-robot distance is non-decreasing in Phase 3 and positions "exponentially stabilize to their goals" (p. 17). Scope caveat, verbatim: "both in simulations and experiments, we observed incidence of only Category A deadlock so our resolution algorithm is specific for this category" (p. 12).
- **Robot counts:** closed-form before-deadlock analysis for 2 and 3 robots (Theorems 1-2); during-deadlock set properties for general N (Sec. 5); resolution demonstrated "on two and three robots in simulation, and experimentally on Khepera-IV robots" (p. 2). Experimental caveat: for the nonholonomic two-robot case "deadlock only occurs if the body frames of both robots are perfectly aligned ... we simulated a virtual deadlock at t = 0" (p. 12), while "In three robots, we did observe deadlock experimentally" (p. 12). Future work: "we want to extend this to N ≥ 4 case" (Sec. 7, p. 14).

### Inferences
- "Deadlock resolution" here is a *control-theoretic exit maneuver with proofs*, categorically different from the VLM-planner papers: no learned/semantic component, no plan object, guarantees limited to N≤3 and one deadlock geometry. Good anchor for the thesis's "classical guaranteed resolution vs. semantic heuristic resolution" axis.

### Gaps
- The published IJRR version (42(6):317-336, 2023) may differ in pagination from the arXiv v1 read here; quotes were taken from arXiv v1.

## 5. Vocabulary sweep: is any existing "intervention/override/takeover rate" formally equivalent to per-leg plan provenance?

### Takeaway
No. All established metrics found — AV disengagement/takeover rates, RL shield intervention rates, CBF safety-filter activation rates — are **per-action, per-timestep, or per-distance runtime filtering statistics on a control stream**; none audits a discrete model-proposed multi-step PLAN and reports the fraction of its legs preserved vs. overridden by a downstream checker. The closest relatives are RL shield-intervention rates (per-action provenance of the executed action) and RoCo's re-plan counts (plan-level, but counts rejection iterations, not preserved fraction).

### Cited Findings
- **AV "disengagement rate":** California DMV defines disengagements as deactivation of autonomous mode "when a failure of the autonomous technology is detected or when the safe operation of the vehicle requires that the autonomous vehicle test driver disengage the autonomous mode and take immediate manual control" — reported as miles-per-disengagement; reported manual takeovers range from ~2x10^-4 to ~3 per mile depending on maker — [Efficient Black-box Assessment of Autonomous Vehicle Safety (arXiv:1912.03618)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.03618). It is a per-distance runtime human-override statistic, and is widely criticized as non-standardized ("There is no accepted standard for when a disengagement should occur nor do AV manufacturers publish their internal definitions") — [EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/disengagements-wrong-metric-for-av-testing/); [Starsky Robotics / Medium](https://medium.com/starsky-robotics-blog/why-miles-per-disengagement-misses-the-point-as-an-autonomous-vehicle-success-metric-c3395cb0a196).
- **RL shielding "shield intervention rate":** in shielded RL "a shield intervenes when it predicts that the action proposed by the agent will transit the environment to an unsafe state", with the "minimum interference" premise; blocked-action feedback is used to "reduce the shield intervention rate in future" — [Fear Field framework, Eng. Appl. of AI 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0952197625000557); shielding under partial observability — [Carr, Jansen et al., AAAI 2023 (arXiv:2204.00755)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.00755). The unit of accounting is the single proposed action at each timestep.
- **Provably-safe RL "failsafe interventions":** "Reducing Safety Interventions in Provably Safe Reinforcement Learning" counts runtime failsafe-controller overrides and notes "While necessary for safety, these interventions can result in undesirable behaviors, such as abrupt braking or aggressive steering", aiming for "a low number of failsafe interventions" — [arXiv:2303.03339](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03339). Again per-runtime-event, not per-plan-element.
- **CBF "safety filter activation":** CBF-QP filters minimally modify a nominal command online ("When the safety filter is activated, it modifies the user command to prevent collision while keeping the modification minimal"); recent work defines a "CBF activation degree" measuring how strongly a learned policy relies on the filter, and tunable filters set "intervention aggressiveness" via a class-K parameter — [Tunable Real-Time Safety Filters via Set-Based CBFs (arXiv:2507.07805)](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.07805); [CBF-informed RL for CAVs (arXiv:2605.16894)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.16894); [Neural CBFs for Safe Navigation (arXiv:2407.19907)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.19907). All per-control-cycle (e.g., 20 Hz) activation statistics.
- **Within the deep-read corpus:** Garg et al. count "number of high-level interventions" (how often the VLM is *called*, i.e., how often the system re-deadlocks — a symptom count, not an override count; pp. 7, 9, 21 of their PDF); RoCo reports "average number of re-plan attempts" per round (whole-plan rejections until a plan passes validation; Table 2, p. 5). Neither decomposes a plan into legs and attributes each leg's provenance.

### Inferences
- A defensible positioning sentence for the thesis: shield-intervention and safety-filter-activation rates are the per-ACTION analogue of plan provenance (each executed action is attributed to "policy" or "shield"); AV disengagement rates are the per-MISSION-TIME analogue; RoCo's re-plan count is a plan-REJECTION iteration count. A per-leg audit of a model-proposed plan — "what fraction of the proposed plan survived the downstream checker vs. was overridden" — does not appear under any of the searched names, so the term can be introduced as new while citing shielding/safety-filter rates as nearest kin and the key difference (runtime stream filtering vs. post-hoc plan-level attribution).

### Gaps
- Searched terms: "shield intervention rate", "safety filter activation rate / intervention rate", "CBF filter activation", "disengagement rate", "takeover rate", "override rate". No source was found using "intervention rate" to mean fraction-of-plan-preserved; absence of evidence after six targeted queries is reported as such, not proof no paper exists. HRI-specific "override rate" (e.g., shared-autonomy teleoperation) was only covered indirectly via the CBF shared-control results; a dedicated HRI sweep was not run.
