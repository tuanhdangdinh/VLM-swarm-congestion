# Reservation- and Schedule-Based Autonomous Intersection Management (AIM) for Four-Way Intersections

Scope: centralized intersection-management literature from Dresner & Stone's AIM onward — reservation granting, conflict/collision models, guarantees, and deadlock/starvation handling — ending with design implications for a warehouse central reasoner that issues one coordination command per analysis cycle.

Sourcing note: Dresner & Stone (JAIR 2008) and Aoki & Rajkumar (A-DRIVE, arXiv 2022) were read in full from the primary PDFs. Khayatian et al.'s ACM TCPS 2020 survey was inaccessible (HTTP 403 at dl.acm.org); claims about it come from its abstract page and from citations in other primary papers. Items marked "(via search summary)" are supported by search-result quotations of the linked source, not by a full read.

## Q1. How does classic AIM (Dresner & Stone 2004/2008) grant/deny reservations, and how are conflicts between perpendicular flows sequenced?

### Takeaway
AIM is a request/response protocol: each approaching vehicle sends a REQUEST (arrival time, arrival velocity, lane, vehicle dimensions, turn intent); the intersection manager runs an *internal simulation* of the vehicle's trajectory across an n×n grid of space-time "reservation tiles" and CONFIRMs iff no simulated tile is already reserved by another vehicle at the same time step — otherwise REJECT, and the vehicle decelerates and retries later. Perpendicular flows are never explicitly "sequenced" as flows: ordering emerges vehicle-by-vehicle from First-Come-First-Served (FCFS) processing of requests against the shared tile map.

### Cited Findings
- The intersection manager mediates between driver agents and a pluggable "intersection control policy"; driver agents send REQUEST / CHANGE-REQUEST / CANCEL / DONE, the manager replies CONFIRM (with reservation ID, start time, start/departure lane, and an acceleration-constraint list) or REJECT, plus ACKNOWLEDGE and a special EMERGENCY-STOP message "only used when the intersection manager detects a major problem in the intersection" — [Dresner & Stone, JAIR 31:591–656, 2008, §3.2](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- FCFS policy mechanics: "The intersection is divided into an n×n grid of reservation tiles… the policy runs an internal simulation of the trajectory of the vehicle across the intersection… If at any time during the simulation the requesting vehicle occupies a reservation tile that is already reserved by another vehicle, the policy rejects the request. Otherwise, the policy accepts the reservation and reserves the appropriate tiles for the times they will be required." — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §3.4 + Fig. 3](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- Granting tries at most two candidate trajectories per request: (1) accelerate to maximum velocity on entry, (2) constant velocity throughout; if neither fits it rejects. Fixed-velocity reservations below 10 m/s are refused so no vehicle "will spend too long in the intersection" hogging space-time — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §3.4.2](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- The intersection manager (not the vehicle) chooses the acceleration schedule and the outbound lane; drivers "may not pilot the vehicle into the intersection without a reservation" and must obey the confirmed constraints — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §2.2/§3.4.1–3.4.2](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- On rejection, the driver agent "decelerates (down to a minimum velocity) and requests again"; a timeout system forbids re-requests until t + min(0.5 s, (t_a − t)/2), so vehicles nearer the intersection may retry more often — an implicit proximity prioritization — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §3.3, §3.4.4](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- Robustness to message loss is by design one-sided: "If a message is sent but not received… the worst thing that can happen is additional delay. No collisions can occur due to lost messages," because space is not assumed reserved until CONFIRM arrives — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §3.2.1](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- Granularity controls interleaving of perpendicular flows: at granularity 1 the whole intersection is one tile (mutual exclusion, like a stop-sign box); at granularity 2, non-turning north/south traffic no longer competes with south/north for the same tiles; high granularity allows dense interleaving of crossing flows — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §4.1](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- The protocol abstraction lets one manager emulate a traffic light or stop sign (TRAFFIC-LIGHT and STOP-SIGN policies), so reservation control "subsumes the most popular current methods of intersection control" — [Dresner & Stone 2008 abstract](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~pstone/Papers/bib2html/b2hd-JAIR08-dresner.html); [Dresner & Stone 2008, §3.5](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- Origin of the line: "A Reservation-Based Intersection Control Mechanism," AAMAS 2004; "Multiagent traffic management: An improved intersection control mechanism," AAMAS 2005 — [UT Austin AIM project page](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~aim/); AAMAS 2005 also cited as ref [26] in [A-DRIVE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04910)

### Inferences
- FCFS-AIM has no notion of a "flow-level" decision (e.g., "N-S goes now"): every grant is a per-vehicle space-time carve-out. A central reasoner that emits one flow-level command per cycle (our M2/M3) is coarser than AIM but analogous to AIM's granularity-1 or light-emulation degenerate modes, which D&S show are strictly dominated in throughput by fine-grained tiles.
- The two-candidate-trajectory trick (max-accel, else constant-v, else reject) is a deliberately cheap admission test — "at most two internal simulations per request" — showing that even the canonical system chose bounded per-request computation over optimality.

### Gaps
- The 2004 AAMAS paper's exact differences from the 2008 JAIR version were not separately verified (JAIR paper states the prototype forbade turning and in-intersection acceleration).

## Q2. What later variants matter: auction/market-based, platoon-based, batch/queue-based, optimization-based (MILP/MPC)?

### Takeaway
Four major descendant families: (a) auction/market mechanisms that replace FCFS ordering with willingness-to-pay (Carlino et al. ITSC 2013; Vasirani & Ossowski JAIR 2012); (b) platoon/batch policies that group compatible (non-conflicting) movements and serve them together, which is where the big capacity wins are (Tachet et al. PLOS ONE 2016); (c) optimization-based scheduling (MILP/dynamic-programming/MPC over conflict zones or conflict points), which trades computation for provably better orderings; (d) synchronous/real-time protocols from the cyber-physical-systems community (STIP/BRIP/CSIP/DSIP, RIM) that emphasize verification and robustness rather than throughput.

### Cited Findings
- Auction-based AIM: Carlino, Boyles & Stone run sealed second-price auctions at intersections to decide "the order in which drivers perform conflicting movements," with a system "wallet" bidding on behalf of human passengers based on trip characteristics, budget, and remaining distance; applied to stop signs, signals, and the reservation protocol — [Carlino et al., ITSC 2013, pp. 529–534](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~aim/papers/ITSC13-dcarlino.pdf)
- Market-inspired reservation assignment where drivers trade with the infrastructure agent in a virtual marketplace (competitive market, prices adjust to demand): Vasirani & Ossowski, "A Market-Inspired Approach for Intersection Management in Urban Road Traffic Networks," JAIR 43:621–659, 2012 — [JAIR PDF](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10759/25695); [arXiv 1401.5851](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.5851) (identification from training knowledge; JAIR volume/pages verified from the JAIR download listing)
- Slot/batch-based control: Tachet et al. compare FAIR (FCFS-like slot assignment), BATCH (slot-based with adaptive platooning of up to N compatible vehicles), and FIXED (signal): "Service rates of BATCH are superior, asymptotically doubling service capacity with respect to FIXED as N increases. By contrast, the capacity of the FAIR strategy is only marginally higher than the one achieved by FIXED" — [Tachet et al., PLOS ONE 11(3):e0149607, 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0149607) (via search summary quoting the paper)
- Platoon-based intersection management strategies grouped and compared in [Platoon-based Cooperative Intersection Management Strategies, IEEE (ITSC-affiliated conference publication) 2020](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9129543/); "virtual platoon" methods map 2-D approaching traffic onto a 1-D virtual queue by distance-to-center so non-conflicting vehicles cross simultaneously (via search summaries; see also [platoon-based IM system for AVs, 2017](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318812785_A_platoon-based_intersection_management_system_for_autonomous_vehicles))
- Optimization-based: a two-level scheme with a 0-1 MILP for vehicle entering-sequence scheduling above a multi-vehicle optimal trajectory layer — [Integrated Schedule and Trajectory Optimization for CAVs in a Conflict Zone, IEEE T-ITS/ITS journal, 2020](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/iel7/6979/4358928/09222573.pdf) (via search summary); MILP intersection control also in [Fayazi & Vahidi line, "Intersection control for automated vehicles with MILP," 2016](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/306024789_Intersection_control_for_automated_vehicles_with_MILP)
- Graph-based passing-order optimization: conflict directed graph + coexisting undirected graph; local optimum via improved depth-first spanning tree, global optimum via minimum clique cover, with a distributed control framework ("virtual platoon" style) — [Chen et al., arXiv:2107.07179, 2021 (rev. 2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07179)
- Time/energy-optimal decentralized coordination with a first-in-first-out crossing order and rear-end + lateral safety constraints at adjacent intersections — [Chalaki & Malikopoulos, arXiv:1911.04082, 2019/2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.04082)
- Real-time/CPS synchronous protocols (all cited as refs [5],[7],[18],[28],[29] in A-DRIVE): Azimi et al. STIP "Stip: Spatio-temporal intersection protocols for autonomous vehicles" (ACM/IEEE ICCPS 2014); Ballroom/BRIP synchronous protocol (RTCSA 2015); Aoki & Rajkumar CSIP configurable synchronous intersection protocol (ERTCSA 2017) and DSIP V2V-based synchronous protocol for mixed traffic (RTCSA 2019) — vehicles cross without stopping by following "a strict but well defined spatio-temporal pattern tailored to each intersection" — [A-DRIVE §V Related Work](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04910)
- Robust variant: RIM, "Robust intersection management for connected autonomous vehicles," Khayatian et al., IEEE RTSS 2018, "extended the system to [be] robust against external disturbances and model mismatches" — cited as ref [27] in [A-DRIVE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04910)
- Taxonomy anchor: Khayatian et al., "A Survey on Intersection Management of Connected Autonomous Vehicles," ACM Trans. on Cyber-Physical Systems 4(4), Art. 48, Aug 2020 — [ACM page](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3407903) (full text 403-blocked; see Gaps)
- Network-level caution: back-pressure/pressure-based policies for reservation intersections exist as an alternative to FCFS ordering — [Boyles group, pressure-based policies for reservation-based intersection control](https://sboyles.github.io/research/backpressure_DTA.pdf)

### Inferences
- The families differ mainly in *who orders the conflicts* (arrival time, money, platoon membership, or a solver) while nearly all keep the same underlying conflict model (space-time exclusivity over tiles/zones/points). Ordering policy and conflict model are separable design axes — directly reusable in our system, where the VLM chooses the ordering and M4 enforces the exclusivity.
- The batch result (BATCH ≈ 2× FIXED, FAIR ≈ FIXED) is the strongest quantitative argument that grouping compatible movements per decision cycle — rather than granting one crossing at a time — is where throughput comes from. That maps directly onto "composite command" territory.

### Gaps
- Khayatian ACM TCPS 2020 survey full text could not be fetched (403 on dl.acm.org and ResearchGate mirrors); its internal taxonomy labels (query-based vs. assignment-based etc.) are therefore not quoted here.
- No accessible primary text for the ITSC 2020 platoon-strategies comparison (IEEE paywall); characterization rests on abstract-level search summaries.

## Q3. How do these works prevent or resolve the "scrum" state (vehicles already mutually blocking inside the box)? Recovery vs. prevention?

### Takeaway
Classic AIM and virtually all optimization/schedule descendants are prevention-only: the invariant "no vehicle enters without a compatible space-time grant" means a mutual block inside the box cannot form under compliant behavior, and the literature explicitly assumes compliance. Works addressing recovery from an already-formed deadlock are rare and recent, and treat it as a fault condition (localization/control/communication failure or geometry too large to sense): A-DRIVE (Aoki & Rajkumar 2022) is the flagship recovery protocol — decentralized yield-and-*recede* (back up) with a cost-based priority rule; the graph-based CPS line (Lin et al. 2019; ACM TCPS 2023) analyzes deadlock *resolvability* formally; Qi et al. (IET ITS) and Jäger & Nebel (IROS 2001) are the mobile-robot-side recovery ancestors.

### Cited Findings
- Prevention inside D&S AIM: an early design that forbade acceleration inside the intersection "had several major flaws, the most severe of which was causing a deadlock scenario as vehicles traversed the intersection more and more slowly, unable to recover from the slightest decelerations"; the fix (manager-chosen acceleration schedules) "eliminates the deadlock scenario… by allowing vehicles to recover from decelerating," and a stopped vehicle "can accelerate back up to a reasonable velocity as it crosses" — i.e., the one deadlock D&S encountered was designed out, not recovered from at runtime — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §3.4.2](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- D&S's only in-box fault handling is the EMERGENCY-STOP broadcast "only used when the intersection manager detects a major problem in the intersection" (Section 5 of the paper covers failure modes) — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §3.2](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- RECOVERY — A-DRIVE (Aoki & Rajkumar, arXiv:2204.04910, Apr 2022; authors at NII Tokyo / CMU): deadlocks form "even when the centralized and decentralized cooperation protocols work properly, due to two reasons: (i) System Failure [control, communication, localization, or time-synchronization failure] and (ii) Large Intersection [geometry exceeds communication/sensing range]." Five concrete scrum scenarios are given, incl. FCFS priority miscalculation where each of 4 vehicles believes it is lower-priority, and stop-line overshoot blocking cross traffic — [A-DRIVE §I–II](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04910)
- A-DRIVE recovery mechanics: vehicles carry 6 states (NOT-AROUND, APPROACH, WAIT, CROSSING, IN-DEADLOCK, YIELDING); each computes a yielding cost χ = s·D_SPACE + t·N_f (distance to the nearest passing place/evacuation space + number of following vehicles), and a threshold waiting time Δ_i = a·χ(v_i) + R_i (R_i random, for ties). Priority rules: (1) vehicles *inside* the intersection outrank those outside; (2) among inside vehicles, larger yielding cost wins; (3) among outside vehicles, random numbers break the tie. Lower-priority vehicles yield **and recede (back up along their recorded path)** to clear the box — [A-DRIVE §III](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04910)
- A-DRIVE explicitly rejects exponential backoff (Ethernet-style) as "neither efficient nor optimal" because backing maneuvers are time- and fuel-costly and a single blocker should yield rather than many — [A-DRIVE §III](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04910)
- A-DRIVE results: vs. a lane-priority baseline (lower-priority lane always backs up), average and worst-case trip delay are lower at 200–800 veh/h and for intersection sizes 10–100 m; baseline recovery time grows steeply ("exponentially" per authors) with intersection length because vehicles "recede multiple times" — [A-DRIVE §IV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04910)
- A-DRIVE degrades gracefully: on packet loss a vehicle is reclassified as non-connected and everyone falls back to the perception-based variant (yield cost signaled implicitly by measured waiting time) — "robust and resilient against packet loss and/or delay, and never compromises road safety" — [A-DRIVE §III](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04910)
- FORMAL ANALYSIS — Lin et al., "Graph-based modeling, scheduling, and verification for intersection management of intelligent vehicles," ACM TECS 18(5s):95, 2019 — "derived formal verification approaches based on graphs and Petri nets for deadlock-free intersection management" — cited as ref [6] in [A-DRIVE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04910)
- FORMAL ANALYSIS — "Graph-Based Deadlock Analysis and Prevention for Robust Intelligent Intersection Management," ACM Trans. on Cyber-Physical Systems (article 3632179, 2023/2024): refines graph-based intersection models "to analyze potential deadlocks and their resolvability," with algorithms "crafted to guarantee deadlock-free operation" — [ACM TCPS](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3632179) (via search summary; full text paywalled)
- RECOVERY (robots) — Jäger & Nebel, "Decentralized collision avoidance, deadlock detection, and deadlock resolution for multiple mobile robots," IROS 2001 — the multi-robot ancestor cited by A-DRIVE as ref [12] — [A-DRIVE references](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04910)
- RECOVERY (mixed traffic) — Qi et al., "Deadlock detection, cooperative avoidance and recovery protocol for mixed autonomous vehicles in unstructured environment," IET Intelligent Transport Systems (2023/2024) — [IET ITS](https://ietresearch.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1049/itr2.12338) (found via search; not read in full)
- Mixed-AV deadlock protocol also in [Intersection Deadlock Protocol on Mixed Autonomous Vehicles, Springer LNNS/CCIS chapter, 2024](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-97-3597-6_13) (not read; noted for completeness)
- Survey-level statement of the requirement: "Deadlocks and starvation should not occur… every vehicle approaching an intersection should eventually cross, even if it is better for the rest of the agents to leave that vehicle stranded"; also, an intersection deadlock "is likely to evolve into a large-scale traffic jam, with normal regulation measures becoming useless" if not unlocked timely — via search summaries of the AIM survey/deadlock literature ([Khayatian survey](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3407903); [ACM TCPS 3632179](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3632179))

### Inferences
- The literature's near-universal position is that a scrum is *out of model*: reservations make it unreachable, so almost nobody plans for it. Our warehouse setting inverts this — the scrum is the observed start state — which places our problem closer to A-DRIVE/Jäger-Nebel (recovery protocols) than to AIM proper, while AIM supplies the post-recovery sequencing discipline.
- A-DRIVE's two-phase logic — first empty the box via cost-ordered receding, then let survivors cross by priority — is structurally identical to the proposed "composite command" (cross-pair reverses out, then the axis pair yields/crosses). A-DRIVE is the strongest available precedent that reverse-out is the correct primitive, and that *the cheapest-to-move vehicle backs up, not everyone*.
- A-DRIVE's priority rule 1 (inside > outside) matches our current "yielder + ordered crossing" primitive only if the yielder is outside the box; when all blockers are inside, its rule 2 (min yielding cost recedes) is the principled selector our M3 grounding currently lacks.

### Gaps
- Could not obtain the full text of either graph-based formal paper (TECS 2019, TCPS 3632179); their exact deadlock-characterization (cycle in a wait-for/conflict graph, Petri-net siphon, etc.) is not confirmed from primary text.
- No work was found that lets a *centralized* manager command a multi-robot reverse-out from a formed scrum; A-DRIVE is decentralized. The centralized-recovery slot appears to be an open niche.

## Q4. What minimum spacing / safety-envelope assumptions do they make, and how do they compare to a hard pairwise 0.45 m centre-to-centre floor?

### Takeaway
AIM-family systems do not use a metric pairwise distance floor at all: safety is *space-time exclusivity* of tiles/zones/conflict points, inflated by buffers. Dresner & Stone's production choice is a **hybrid buffer** — a time buffer that scales with vehicle speed (covering early/late arrival) plus a small static spatial buffer for lateral error — plus enlarged "edge tiles" enforcing a 1-second following interval on intersection exit. Time-headway-at-conflict-point separation, not distance, is the standard safety envelope; a hard center-to-center floor like ours (0.45 m) is closer to the CPS/robotics safety-invariant style than anything in the AIM canon.

### Cited Findings
- Static buffers alone rejected: assuming each vehicle 10× its size "certainly suffice[s] for safety purposes" but "defeats the point of the intersection manager"; pure time buffers alone are "not sufficient to guarantee safety — a small error in lateral positioning… may still cause a collision"; the adopted hybrid buffer "has a time buffer that scales with velocity, as well as a small static buffer that protects against lateral positioning errors and serves as a minimum buffer for slow-moving vehicles" — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §3.4.5 + Fig. 5](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- Edge tiles: following intervals inside the box are deliberately *not* enforced (the manager "may orchestrate some 'close calls'"), but tiles abutting the intersection boundary get their time buffer raised to the desired following interval (example: 1 second) so vehicles exiting in the same lane keep a gap; internal tiles keep small buffers so perpendicular interleaving stays tight — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §3.4.6 + Fig. 6](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- On the open road D&S driver agents keep "a following distance of one second" (time headway, hence speed-scaled) — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §3.3](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- Discretization is acknowledged as a safety hazard: "artifacts resulting from the discretization of time are enough to weaken the reservation tiles' guarantees of exclusivity," motivating the buffers — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §3.4.5](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- Optimization-line envelopes: Malikopoulos-style decentralized coordination imposes explicit rear-end collision constraints (minimum inter-vehicle gap along a lane) and lateral collision avoidance by time-separating occupancy of the merging/conflict zone — [Chalaki & Malikopoulos, arXiv:1911.04082](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.04082); MILP schedulers assign conflict-zone entry times so that occupancy intervals at shared conflict points never overlap — [Integrated Schedule and Trajectory Optimization, 2020](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/iel7/6979/4358928/09222573.pdf) (via search summary)
- A-DRIVE's safety floor during recovery is perception-based: "each vehicle always uses its own perception systems and cannot proceed to avoid vehicle collisions" — i.e., local sensing overrides the protocol, and messages use SAE J2735 BSM at 10 Hz with 400 m V2V range in evaluation — [A-DRIVE §III–IV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04910)
- Robustness-oriented CPS variants exist precisely because nominal AIM buffers assume near-perfect tracking: RIM (RTSS 2018) targets "external disturbances and model mismatches"; the synchronous protocols (STIP/BRIP/CSIP/DSIP) use fixed spatio-temporal crossing patterns with verified separation — [A-DRIVE §V citations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04910)

### Inferences
- Translating to our system: AIM's envelope is *conditional* on commanded trajectories (space-time), while our 0.45 m floor is *unconditional* (any pair, any time). The unconditional floor is strictly stronger and simpler to monitor (M4 already enforces it), but it forbids the "close calls" from which AIM extracts its throughput; expect our achievable crossing density to be closer to AIM-at-coarse-granularity than to granularity-24 AIM.
- D&S's hybrid buffer suggests a cheap upgrade path for us if 0.45 m ever binds too hard during recovery: keep the static floor for near-stationary robots but let the effective envelope grow with commanded speed rather than staying constant — exactly the static+time decomposition.
- Their edge-tile lesson transfers directly: the danger zone is the *exit* of the conflict area (a robot clearing the box slowly while the next command launches a crosser behind it). A per-exit-lane extra time margin on top of the pairwise floor is the AIM-tested fix.

### Gaps
- No AIM paper found that states a numeric center-to-center or bumper-to-bumper distance floor comparable to 0.45 m; comparisons above are structural, not numeric. (Vehicle-scale numbers wouldn't transfer to 0.3–0.5 m warehouse robots anyway.)

## Q5. What is reported about throughput vs. fairness/starvation at four-way junctions?

### Takeaway
FCFS reservations crush signals at a single intersection (D&S report delay reductions up to two orders of magnitude), FCFS is starvation-free by construction and widely used as the fairness proxy — but Levin & Boyles proved FCFS reservations can be *worse than signals* in networks (paradoxes from disrupted platoon progression and priority inversions), and Tachet et al. showed the FCFS-like FAIR policy gains almost no capacity over signals while batching compatible movements doubles it. The field's consistent message: fairness-by-arrival-order and throughput pull in opposite directions, and the winning compromise is batching with bounded batch size.

### Cited Findings
- "The reservation system can reduce delay by two orders of magnitude" vs. traffic lights and stop signs across traffic levels, granularities, and human-driver fractions — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §4](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- D&S anti-starvation mechanisms inside FCFS: (a) the timeout formula gives stopped-at-the-line vehicles unlimited retry frequency while distant vehicles wait; (b) the *reservation distance* heuristic — per-lane variable d_i; once a request is rejected, later requests in that lane with larger reservation distance are auto-rejected, "giving the [frontmost] vehicle exclusive access (within the lane) to the reservation mechanism" until it gets through, curing the head-of-line starvation where trailing vehicles kept stealing the space-time the leader needed ("a single vehicle can continue for quite some time to obtain reservations that prevent the front car from crossing") — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §3.4.3–3.4.4 + Fig. 4](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- FCFS as fairness proxy: "Because of its inherent fairness, FCFS is a good candidate for a widely accepted control policy… frequently used in autonomous intersection management, often as a proxy for fairness," and there is "no risk of starvation" under FCFS — via search summaries across the reservation literature ([Levin & Boyles line](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965856416303822); [signal-insights follow-up](https://www.tandfonline.com/eprint/k4Z63qpM5SctMPUxim6m/full?target=10.1080/15472450.2018.1519706))
- The paradox result: Levin, Boyles & Patel identified theoretical and real-network scenarios (five, incl. two real networks) "in which traffic signals cause lower delays than FCFS reservations," because signals allocate capacity asymmetrically and protect arterial progression, whereas FCFS lets "vehicles with lower priority and fewer conflict limitations move before higher priority vehicles" (e.g., a local road interrupting an arterial at high demand) — [Paradoxes of reservation-based intersection controls in traffic networks, Transportation Research Part A 90:14–25, 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965856416303822) (via search summary of the abstract)
- Capacity vs. fairness quantified: FAIR (FCFS slot assignment) capacity ≈ signal capacity; BATCH (platoon up to N compatible vehicles per slot decision) asymptotically 2× signal capacity, with dramatically lower delay for both slot policies at moderate demand — [Tachet et al., PLOS ONE 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0149607) (via search summary)
- Auctions explicitly trade fairness for expressed value-of-time, raising equity concerns handled via the system-wallet design (bidding on behalf of all, budget-capped) — [Carlino et al., ITSC 2013](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~aim/papers/ITSC13-dcarlino.pdf)
- Emergency-vehicle priority without starving civilians: FCFS-EMERG grants reservations only to lanes containing an approaching emergency vehicle (whole-lane priority, so the lane drains), rather than denying all civilian requests — priority "without significant cost to civilian vehicles" — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §3.7.2](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590)
- Fairness is an active 2025 topic: a real-time hierarchical control framework for intersections explicitly co-optimizing fairness and safety appeared Nov 2025 — [Fair and Safe: A Real-Time Hierarchical Control Framework for Intersections, arXiv:2511.05886](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.05886) (post-training-data item surfaced by search; not read in full — title/abstract only)
- Aggregate delay figures at four-way junctions under recovery load (A-DRIVE): average trip delay grows from ~2 s at 200 veh/h to ~10 s (A-DRIVE) vs ~13 s (baseline) at 800 veh/h; worst-case trip delay ~6 s vs ~30 s — read off Fig. 7 of [A-DRIVE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04910)

### Inferences
- For a warehouse M5 that cares about task completion, the Levin-Boyles paradox is a warning against pure arrival-order tie-breaking in M3: a robot with many downstream conflicts should not automatically outrank a robot whose crossing conflicts with nothing (our "ordered crossing" should order by conflict footprint, not just detection order).
- The two proven anti-starvation devices — per-lane head-of-line exclusivity (D&S reservation distance) and whole-lane draining for priority flows (FCFS-EMERG) — are both single-scalar rules cheap enough to encode as M3 grounding constraints rather than VLM decisions.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures fairness/starvation specifically for *warehouse robots* at four-way aisle crossings; all numbers are vehicle-traffic simulations.
- Tachet's FAIR/BATCH capacities are for a simplified queue-theoretic intersection model, not a geometric simulation; transferability of the exact 2× factor is untested.

## Design implications for our system (one coordination command per analysis cycle; primitive = one yielder + ordered crossing; candidate extension = composite reverse-then-yield command)

### Takeaway
The literature validates the composite command: recovery-capable systems (A-DRIVE) are exactly "cheapest blockers recede, then survivors cross in priority order," and batch scheduling (Tachet BATCH) shows that grouping compatible crossings into one decision roughly doubles service rate over one-at-a-time granting. The pieces the canon says to keep out of the LLM and put in deterministic grounding: inside-outranks-outside, cheapest-to-move-yields, head-of-line exclusivity per lane, and exit-side spacing margins.

### Cited Findings
- (All supporting citations above; key anchors:) A-DRIVE's recede-then-cross with cost-ordered yielder selection and inside>outside priority — [A-DRIVE §III](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04910); BATCH's compatible-group service doubling capacity vs. one-slot-at-a-time FAIR — [Tachet et al. 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0149607); D&S deadlock designed out by guaranteeing forward progress (acceleration allowed, min crossing speed, EMERGENCY-STOP as last resort) — [Dresner & Stone 2008, §3.4.2](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10542/25241/19590); FCFS paradoxes → order by conflict footprint — [Levin & Boyles 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965856416303822)

### Inferences
1. **Composite command is precedented, with a specific shape.** A-DRIVE's protocol is the literature's answer to a formed scrum: select the vehicle(s) with the *lowest yielding cost* (shortest reverse to a free pocket + fewest robots queued behind it) to recede; everyone still inside then crosses before anyone outside enters. Our composite "cross-pair reverses out, then axis pair yields" should ground the reverse-pair selection on an A-DRIVE-style cost (reverse distance + followers), not on axis identity alone — and prefer a *single* receding robot when one suffices, since multi-robot backing is the expensive branch A-DRIVE explicitly avoids.
2. **One command per cycle ≠ one crossing per cycle.** Tachet's FAIR-vs-BATCH gap says a command that releases a *set* of mutually non-conflicting crossings (e.g., both N→S and S→N straights) buys ~2× service rate at zero safety cost, because compatibility is checkable deterministically by M3/M4 against the pairwise floor. The VLM should pick the *batch axis/order*; grounding should expand it to all compatible members.
3. **Encode the two liveness rules from D&S as M3 gate invariants:** (a) head-of-line exclusivity — never grant a crossing to a robot whose lane leader is still blocked (reservation-distance rule); (b) bounded re-request/timeout so a repeatedly-denied robot's priority effectively rises with waiting time. Both are one-scalar bookkeeping, ideal for the deterministic layer; leaving them to per-cycle VLM judgment reintroduces the starvation FCFS-AIM had to patch.
4. **Guarantee forward progress inside the conflict zone.** D&S's one observed deadlock came from robots being unable to speed back up inside the box. Corollary for us: a crossing command must permit re-acceleration after safety slowdowns (M4 collision-monitor holds must not latch), and a minimum-crossing-speed / maximum-dwell rule inside the aisle box is the AIM-tested way to stop a slow crosser from consuming the whole cycle.
5. **Exit-side margin, not just in-box margin.** Edge-tile logic: the next crossing batch should be gated on the previous crosser being clear of the exit lane by a time margin, not merely outside the box polygon — with a hard 0.45 m floor this is where the residual collision risk concentrates.
6. **Recovery is the under-served niche.** Every centralized work found is prevention-only; centralized *commanded* recovery from a formed scrum (our M2→M3 pipeline) has no direct precedent — the closest are decentralized (A-DRIVE, Jäger & Nebel 2001). This is both a citation gap to state honestly in any write-up and a defensible novelty claim for the VLM-swarm paper.
7. **Failure semantics matter to the primitive set.** A-DRIVE classifies deadlocks as products of *failures* (localization overshoot, priority miscalculation, sensing range). Our M1 STALE/overlap findings mirror this: the composite command should be triggered by M5's congestion-timeout (deadlock detector), and its grounding should tolerate one non-compliant robot (A-DRIVE's fallback: treat it as non-connected, plan around it) rather than assuming all four robots execute.

### Gaps
- No literature guidance found on LLM/VLM-issued intersection commands (the field assumes numeric schedulers); nothing to cite on per-cycle command-rate limits like ours.
- Open question the canon doesn't answer: when *both* members of the cross-pair must reverse (no single-yielder resolution exists), no source quantifies coordinated simultaneous backing vs. sequential backing — A-DRIVE just says multi-vehicle backing is "difficult and time- and fuel-consuming." Sequential (one command per cycle) is thus not obviously worse and is the safer default given our 0.45 m floor.
