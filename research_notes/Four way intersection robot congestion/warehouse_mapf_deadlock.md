# Multi-Robot Warehouse Coordination and Junction Deadlock Resolution: MAPF and Priority-Based Methods

Research notes, 2026-09-29. Scope: how warehouse/AMR systems prevent and resolve junction deadlocks — lifelong MAPF (RHCR), priority/rule-based traffic control, zone locking, and explicitly deadlock DETECTION + RECOVERY. Papers giving an explicit un-jamming procedure or withdrawal ordering are flagged **[UN-JAM]**.

## KQ1: How do lifelong MAPF systems (RHCR, Li et al.) handle intersections — do formed deadlocks occur, and what recovery exists when execution deviates from plan?

### Takeaway
In the MAPF paradigm, junction deadlocks are prevented at *plan time*, not resolved at *run time*: a valid MAPF plan has no vertex/swap conflicts, so a physically formed jam is impossible as long as the plan (or its precedence ordering) is respected. Deadlock appears in two other guises — planner-level livelock/starvation when the planning window is too short (RHCR Example 2), and execution-time deadlock risk when robots deviate from plan timing, which is handled by Action-Dependency-Graph execution layers that preserve the planned passing order rather than by any retreat maneuver. Formed jams are assumed away; the literature contains essentially no back-out recovery inside the MAPF stack itself.

### Cited Findings
- MAPF definitions: agents move on a graph in discrete timesteps; a collision is a *vertex conflict* (same location, same timestep) or a *swapping conflict* (traverse same edge in opposite directions); a MAPF plan is a set of collision-free paths — Stern et al., "Multi-Agent Pathfinding: Definitions, Variants, and Benchmarks," SoCS 2019 [SoCS proceedings](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/SOCS/article/view/18510), [arXiv:1906.08291](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08291); definitions restated in [RHCR, AAAI 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07371).
- RHCR (Rolling-Horizon Collision Resolution), Li, Tinka, Kiesel, Durham, Kumar, Koenig, AAAI 2021: decomposes lifelong MAPF into Windowed MAPF instances; collisions resolved only within time horizon *w*; replanning every *h* timesteps (*w ≥ h*); scales to 1,000 agents (= 38.9% of empty map cells) on simulated warehouse maps — [AAAI 2021 paper](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/17344), [arXiv:2005.07371](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07371), [code](https://github.com/Jiaoyang-Li/RHCR).
- Bounded horizons *create* deadlocks (really livelocks): RHCR Example 2 shows two agents in a corridor with w=2 that "find the same paths for both agents again and force them to wait for two more timesteps. Overall, the agents wait at cells B and C forever and never reach their goal locations" — [RHCR §4.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07371).
- RHCR's deadlock-avoidance mechanism (§4.4): a potential function P(w) counts agents that made progress toward goals within the window; if P(w) < p (user threshold), the horizon w is *increased* and the Windowed MAPF solver re-run until P(w) ≥ p — i.e., the recovery lever is "plan farther ahead," not "back a robot out" — [RHCR §4.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07371).
- Even with that mechanism, RHCR is incomplete, and the failure case is precisely an intersection: "Imagine an intersection where many agents are moving horizontally but only one agent wants to move vertically. If we always let the horizontal agents move and the vertical agent wait, we maximize the throughput but lose completeness (as the vertical agent can never reach its goal location) … This issue can occur even if we use time horizon w = ∞. Since throughput and completeness compete with each other, we choose to focus on throughput" — [RHCR §4.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07371). So starvation at junctions is an acknowledged, accepted failure mode of throughput-optimized lifelong MAPF.
- Execution deviation: RHCR explicitly "assume[s] that the drive units can execute any MAPF plan perfectly," delegating robustness to post-processing (Hönig et al. 2016, kinematic constraints) and to the framework of Hönig et al. 2019 that "interleaves planning and execution and can be directly incorporated into RHCR" — [RHCR §3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07371).
- Hönig, Kiesel, Tinka, Durham, Ayanian, "Persistent and Robust Execution of MAPF Schedules in Warehouses," IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters 4(2):1125–1131, 2019 (Amazon Robotics co-authors): the Action Dependency Graph (ADG) encodes every action of every robot as a vertex with Type-1 edges (a robot's own action order) and Type-2 edges (cross-robot precedences, e.g., "robot B may enter cell c only after robot A has left it"); actions execute only when prerequisites complete, guaranteeing collision-free execution under "unforeseen robot slow-downs" and time-varying dynamics without global re-synchronization — [RA-L PDF](https://whoenig.github.io/publications/2019_RA-L_Hoenig.pdf), [Amazon Science page](https://www.amazon.science/publications/persistent-and-robust-execution-of-mapf-schedules-in-warehouses).
- Delay-induced blocking under ADG: because passing order at a junction is frozen at plan time, a badly delayed robot stalls everyone ordered behind it. The Switchable ADG (SADG) feedback scheme re-orders these cross-robot dependencies online ("which AGV goes first through a shared cell") via a shrinking-horizon MILP "while guaranteeing collision- and deadlock-free plan execution," cutting cumulative route completion times when AGVs are delayed — Berndt, Duijkeren, Palmieri, Keviczky, [arXiv:2010.05254](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.05254) (2020; later ICRA/journal versions); streamlined version [arXiv:2412.01277](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.01277) (2024).
- Well-formed infrastructures: Čáp, Vokřínek, Kleiner (ICAPS 2015) define maps where every pair of task endpoints is connected by a path avoiding all other endpoints; on such maps, prioritized "backtrack-free" planning is complete and agents can always be routed to rest at endpoints — summarized with citation in [RHCR §2.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07371). Alternatives on non-well-formed maps: force agents to "hold" goal locations (Ma et al., AAMAS 2017) or plan "dummy paths" to safe locations (Liu et al., AAMAS 2019); both reduce throughput (RHCR Table 1: holding endpoints −4.3 to −6.8% throughput vs RHCR) — [RHCR §2.2, §5.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07371).
- The Kiva/Amazon fulfillment map used in RHCR experiments is the standard testbed (Fig. 1a "borrowed from Wurman, D'Andrea, and Mountz 2007"), i.e., this whole literature is aimed at Kiva-style grid warehouses with orthogonal junctions — [RHCR Fig. 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07371).
- A 2026 follow-on line learns *when* to replan during robust MAPF execution ("Should I Replan? Learning to Spot the Right Time in Robust MAPF Execution," [arXiv:2604.25567](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.25567)) — evidence that delay-handling (not jam recovery) remains the active question in MAPF execution.

### Inferences
- MAPF systems never face a *formed* four-way jam under nominal operation because mutual exclusion is compiled into the plan; the entire "recovery" machinery (ADG/SADG) exists to preserve that invariant under timing noise. Recovery by physical retreat is absent from this literature — if a robot ends up somewhere the plan never authorized (localization fault, manual intervention, dropped robot), the MAPF stack has no primitive for it; that gap is exactly what A-DRIVE-style protocols (KQ4) and our yield primitive address.
- RHCR's admission that intersection starvation is accepted for throughput implies a deployed system needs an out-of-band fairness/aging mechanism at junctions (as our M5 timeout-triggered escalation effectively is).

### Gaps
- No published account found of what Amazon Robotics does *operationally* when a drive unit physically blocks a junction outside plan (fault case); Hönig et al. 2019 handles slow-downs, not off-plan positions.
- I did not find a lifelong-MAPF paper that measures frequency of execution-time deadlocks on real robots (only simulated delay models).

## KQ2: Classical deadlock taxonomy for multi-robot systems (wait-for graphs, cycle detection) and which recovery actions are used

### Takeaway
The field inherits the OS taxonomy — prevention / avoidance / detection+recovery, with deadlock = cycle in a wait-for (resource-allocation) graph — and multi-robot recovery actions cluster into four families: (a) successive replanning of one blocker at a time (Jäger & Nebel), (b) edge-reversal / passing-order reassignment (SADG, coordination graphs), (c) physical retreat/back-off of selected robots (A-DRIVE, spare-point methods), and (d) perturbation/leader-election to escape force-equilibrium deadlocks in continuous controllers (Grover et al., foundation-model leader selection).

### Cited Findings
- Wait-for graph: system state as directed graph of "process i waits for resource held by j"; deadlock iff the graph contains a cycle; detection = periodic cycle search — [Wikipedia: Wait-for graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wait-for_graph); classic treatment R.C. Holt, "Some deadlock properties of computer systems," ACM Computing Surveys 4(3):179–196, 1972 (cited as ref [13] in [A-DRIVE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.04910)); distributed-systems version M. Singhal, "Deadlock detection in distributed systems," IEEE Computer 22(11):37–48, 1989 (A-DRIVE ref [22]).
- **[UN-JAM]** Jäger & Nebel, "Decentralized collision avoidance, deadlock detection, and deadlock resolution for multiple mobile robots," IROS 2001, pp. 1213–1219 — per Grover et al.'s survey, "the first to propose algorithms for deadlock resolution specifically for multiple mobile robots." Mechanism: when two robots come within a threshold distance they exchange planned trajectories; potential collisions are avoided by inserting idle times between trajectory segments; a deadlock is detected when robots block each other so none can proceed without collision; resolution: "the trajectory planners of each of the involved robots are successively asked to plan an alternative trajectory until the deadlock is resolved" — i.e., a sequential, one-robot-at-a-time replan ordering — [IROS 2001 PDF](https://gki.informatik.uni-freiburg.de/papers/jaeger-nebel-iros01.pdf), [IEEE](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/977148/).
- One search-result description of wait-for-graph-based multi-robot resolution (traffic module docs): "Initially an attempt is made to change the direction of one edge in the circle, since this would destroy the circle and therefore resolve the deadlock. If no edge direction can be changed, robots are chosen and asked to plan alternative trajectories" — [Waremax deadlock docs](https://docs.skelfresearch.com/waremax/concepts/traffic/deadlock/) (industrial documentation, not peer-reviewed; corroborates the edge-reversal-then-replan hierarchy).
- Grover, Liu, Sycara, "The Before, During and After of Multi-Robot Deadlock," International Journal of Robotics Research (paper formatted for IJRR; [arXiv:2206.01781](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.01781), 2022) — the most complete formal treatment for continuous-space robots. Structure follows the deadlock chronology: *Before* (which initial conditions/controllers inevitably deadlock), *During* (geometric properties of the deadlocked state), *After* (provably-correct decentralized exit controllers, validated on Khepera-IV robots).
  - Definition: robot i is in deadlock if its QP/CBF-filtered control u\*_i = 0 while the nominal controller û_i ≠ 0 and the robot is not at its goal (Def. 1).
  - "System deadlock is characterized by a force-equilibrium on robots": attraction to goal exactly balanced by resultant repulsion from *active* neighbors (KKT/duality interpretation, Eq. 46).
  - "Deadlock occurs to ensure safety when safety is at the brink of being violated"; the deadlock set is "non-empty, bounded and located on the boundary of the safety set."
  - Theorem 3 ("Safety Margin Apart"): two robots in deadlock sit *exactly* the safety distance D_s apart — a formed CBF deadlock leaves zero spatial slack.
  - Geometric symmetry in initial positions/goals *necessarily* causes deadlock (Theorems 1–2 for 2 and 3 robots); heterogeneous controller gains are not sufficient to avoid it.
  - Admissible deadlock configurations grow combinatorially: exact counts {1, 1, 4, 18} for N = 1..4 robots; lower bound 0.5(N+1)!(N−1)! for N ≥ 3 — so analysis (and tailored resolution) beyond 3–4 robots is intractable, motivating generic exit procedures.
  - Related-work classification therein: Duhaut et al. 2007 showed deadlock cannot be avoided without sharing global information and proposed "a random move + priority-based rule for deadlock resolution"; Y. Li, Gupta, Payandeh 2005 "resolve deadlocks in robots navigating through narrow corridors" via coordination graphs (also A-DRIVE ref [24], ICRA 2005); Rodríguez-Seda et al. 2016 add perturbation terms; Zhou et al. 2017 stop robots preemptively (avoidance); velocity-obstacle and RVO methods shown empirically deadlock-prone in symmetric scenes (Douthwaite et al. 2018/2019) — all as surveyed in [arXiv:2206.01781 §2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.01781).
- Symmetry as root cause is its own thread: "Why Does Symmetry Cause Deadlocks?" IFAC World Congress 2020 — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405896320334042).
- Banker's-algorithm adaptations: banker's-style maximum-claims admission used for AGV deadlock avoidance in bidirectional guide-path systems — "Simulation-based Deadlock Avoidance and Optimization in Bidirectional AGVS" [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.5555/2151054.2151085); modern OS-side optimization DBDAA 2024 [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11414889/).
- Plan-repair line: "Deadlock-Free Online Plan Repair in Multi-robot Coordination with Disturbances," FLAIRS — repairs executing multi-robot plans under disturbances while preserving deadlock-freeness — [FLAIRS PDF](https://journals.flvc.org/FLAIRS/article/download/128371/130116/218227).
- **[UN-JAM]** Garg, Zhang, Arkin, Fan, "Foundation Models to the Rescue: Deadlock Resolution in Connected Multi-Robot Systems," [arXiv:2404.06413](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.06413) (2024): connected multi-robot systems "get stuck away from their desired locations under a smooth low-level control policy"; an LLM/VLM high-level planner performs *leader selection* and assigns the leader a waypoint sequence; a GNN-based distributed low-level controller executes it safely; foundation models beat grid-based high-level planners on goal-reaching rate and computation time in obstacle-dense environments. Directly analogous to a VLM choosing which robot yields.
- Adaptive switching of resolution: CBF-inspired risk measurement lets each agent switch deadlock-resolution behavior on/off via a deadlock indicator function — [arXiv:2503.09621](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.09621) (2025).

### Inferences
- Recovery-action taxonomy synthesized from the above: (1) *temporal* — reinsert idle time / reorder passing sequence (cheapest; works only if a feasible ordering still exists, i.e., jam not yet physically formed); (2) *replan* — one blocker at a time takes an alternative route (Jäger & Nebel); (3) *spatial retreat* — selected robots physically back out to free resources (A-DRIVE, spare points; the only option once robots are mutually wedged with no alternative arcs); (4) *symmetry breaking* — perturbation, random move, priority/leader election (needed because symmetric jams are provably stable equilibria). Our yield primitive is family (3) selected by family (4).
- Grover et al.'s Theorem 3 (deadlocked robots sit exactly at the safety margin) formalizes why a formed jam cannot be resolved by forward motion or rotation-in-place alone: any forward component violates the safety floor, so at least one robot must move *away* (retreat) — theoretical backing for a back-out-along-arrival-arm primitive.

### Gaps
- Duhaut et al. 2007 and Y. Li et al. 2005 are cited second-hand via Grover et al.'s related-work section; I did not fetch the primaries.
- No multi-robot paper found that adapts the full OS detection algorithm suite (e.g., probe-based distributed detection) to warehouse fleets in a peer-reviewed venue; industrial docs (Waremax) suggest it is done in practice but unpublished.

## KQ3: Rule/priority schemes governing junction access in deployed warehouse fleets (Kiva/Amazon, AutoStore-style, port AGVs)

### Takeaway
Deployed fleets overwhelmingly use *centralized resource reservation* (path/zone locking) rather than negotiation: Kiva/Amazon reserves paths centrally for 500+ vehicles; industrial AGV practice partitions the floor into zones with mutual exclusion (one vehicle per zone) plus deadlock *prevention* logic (banker's-style admission, grid-block advance reservation at container terminals); recent academic-industrial work layers hierarchical traffic management on top. Published detail on recovery-from-formed-jams in deployed systems is thin — prevention is the deployed doctrine.

### Cited Findings
- Wurman, D'Andrea, Mountz, "Coordinating Hundreds of Cooperative, Autonomous Vehicles in Warehouses," AI Magazine 29(1), 2008 (Kiva Systems): "a central computer that would wirelessly command all the robots in real time"; large installations run 500+ vehicles; movable shelving brought to stationary pickers, productivity ×2 — [AI Magazine PDF](https://www.dcsc.tudelft.nl/~sc4081/2018/assign/pap/robert_kiva_paper.pdf), [AAAI page](https://ojs.aaai.org/aimagazine/index.php/aimagazine/article/view/2082); engineering context in [IEEE Spectrum, "Three Engineers, Hundreds of Robots, One Warehouse," 2008](https://spectrum.ieee.org/three-engineers-hundreds-of-robots-one-warehouse). Later confirmation that "large-scale commercial implementations such as Amazon's Kiva warehouse robots rely on efficient coordination mechanisms to plan and adjust robot paths in milliseconds" (same search corpus).
- Zone control: "Zone-Control-Based Traffic Control of Automated Guided Vehicles" — floor partitioned into zones; each vehicle "freely choose[s] its routes for any finite sequence of zone-to-zone transportation tasks … constructed in an online fashion" with zone mutual exclusion for collision/deadlock safety — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/283021809_Zone-Control-Based_Traffic_Control_of_Automated_Guided_Vehicles).
- Li, Pogromsky, Adriaansen, Udding, "A Control of Collision and Deadlock Avoidance for Automated Guided Vehicles with a Fault-Tolerance Capability," International Journal of Advanced Robotic Systems, 2016 — zone-based traffic control that stays collision/deadlock-free even when a vehicle faults (relevant: fault tolerance is the case MAPF ignores) — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.5772/62685).
- Hierarchical Traffic Management of Multi-AGV Systems With Deadlock Prevention Applied to Industrial Environments (2023): three-layer architecture — Topological Layer (traffic among areas), Middle Layer (path planning), Bottom Layer (final routes + temporal coordination) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/371019271_Hierarchical_Traffic_Management_of_Multi-AGV_Systems_With_Deadlock_Prevention_Applied_to_Industrial_Environments).
- Improved Dynamic Resource Reservation (IDRR) for multi-AGV traffic: "time-efficient task completion and deadlock-free movements" via dynamic reservation of route resources — IEEE Transactions paper, 2024 — [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10419190/).
- **[UN-JAM, partial]** "Multi-AGVs Collision-Avoidance and Deadlock-Control for Item-To-Human Automated Warehouse" (IEEE ICIT-region conference, 2017): dynamic spare-point strategy — AGVs record predecessor/successor and reserved points, "only entering sequential shared points if their application is successful, otherwise stopping to avoid causing an unresolvable deadlock"; when conflict occurs one AGV is diverted to a *spare point* (a designated evacuation cell), which is a retreat-to-refuge recovery — [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7985596).
- Port AGVs (automated container terminals): (a) "Deadlock prevention for automated guided vehicles in automated container terminals" (OR Spectrum 28, 2006): travel area divided into grid-blocks "reserved in advance when AGVs are running… the first purpose of the reservation being to make room between AGVs and to prevent deadlocks" — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-49550-5_12), [OR Spectrum](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00291-006-0055-2); (b) "Deadlock handling for real-time control of AGVs at automated container terminals" (OR Spectrum 28, 2006) — companion work on real-time detection/handling — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00291-006-0053-4).
- Review: "Analysis of Multi-AGVs Management System and Key Issues: A Review" (CMES 2022) — three causes of multi-AGV deadlock: "insufficient system resources, improper dispatch sequence, and improper resource allocation" — [TechScience](https://www.techscience.com/CMES/v131n3/47394/html).
- Distributed alternative: "Distributed Collision-free Protocol for AGVs in Industrial Environments" — decentralized zone-negotiation protocol ([arXiv:1101.2270](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1101.2270)).
- Simulation infrastructure for Kiva-style systems: RAWSim-O simulation framework for robotic mobile fulfillment systems ([arXiv:1710.04726](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.04726)); decision-rule studies ([arXiv:1801.06703](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.06703)) — these model path reservation and waiting rules at grid junctions.

### Inferences
- The industrial invariant everywhere is "never let the jam form": advance reservation of the junction cell(s) with strict one-holder semantics. Deployed systems therefore rarely publish recovery procedures — a formed four-way jam implies the reservation layer already failed (fault, localization error, human in loop), which is precisely the regime our VLM-triggered recovery targets and where academic ITS work (A-DRIVE) is the closest published analogue.
- The spare-point/evacuation-cell idea (item-to-human warehouse paper; also A-DRIVE's "passing places… evacuation space" for single-track lanes) is the industrial cousin of our per-arm retreat: retreat targets are pre-surveyed cells, not ad-hoc positions.

### Gaps
- AutoStore-style (grid-on-top-of-bins) traffic control: no peer-reviewed description found in this pass; AutoStore's controller design appears to be proprietary/unpublished. Patents on AGV zone control exist (e.g., zone-controller claims referenced in industrial literature) but I did not verify a specific Amazon/Kiva traffic patent number, so none is cited.
- No public post-mortem of a real Amazon junction gridlock incident was found (e.g., only journalistic accounts of fire-suppression incident exist; not cited as not relevant/verified).

## KQ4: Literature on multi-robot "un-jamming" — sequenced retreats from a mutual block, and in what order blockers should withdraw

### Takeaway
The only literature giving a complete, explicit withdrawal-ordering rule for formed junction jams is A-DRIVE (Aoki & Rajkumar, IEEE IV 2022): robots *inside* the junction get priority (they cross; outsiders back up), among insiders the one with the *largest yielding cost* stays while cheaper-to-move robots recede first, ties among outsiders break randomly, retreats are staggered in time by cost-proportional wait thresholds (implicitly sequencing the backups), each yielder "simply go[es] back the way [it] came" along its arrival path, and single-robot retreats are preferred over multi-robot ones. Everything else in the space either reorders crossing sequence before the jam forms (SADG), replans one robot at a time (Jäger & Nebel), or perturbs/elects a leader out of a force equilibrium (Grover; foundation models).

### Cited Findings
All A-DRIVE findings from [Aoki & Rajkumar, "A-DRIVE: Autonomous Deadlock Detection and Recovery at Road Intersections for Connected and Automated Vehicles," IEEE Intelligent Vehicles Symposium (IV) 2022, arXiv:2204.04910](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.04910) (read in full):
- **[UN-JAM]** Deadlock definition: "a state where each vehicle is waiting for another vehicle around and/or inside shared road segments, such as four-way stationary intersections, merging points, single-track lanes, and construction zones. To recover from the vehicle deadlock, vehicles have to break a tie and/or at least one vehicle has to yield and recede."
- Deadlocks occur *even when cooperative intersection protocols work correctly*, from (i) system failure (control/communication/localization/time-sync — e.g., FCFS tie miscalculation where each vehicle believes it is lower-priority, or overshooting the stop line) and (ii) large intersections exceeding sensing/communication range. I.e., formed deadlocks are treated as inevitable residue of any prevention layer.
- Six-state machine per vehicle: NOT-AROUND-INTERSECTION → APPROACH → WAIT → CROSSING, plus IN-DEADLOCK and YIELDING; WAIT and CROSSING vehicles continuously self-monitor (local perception + wait time) to detect deadlock.
- Priority rules for recovery (Section III.A.3, verbatim structure): "(1) Vehicles within the intersection (ρ_location = 1) have the higher priority, compared to vehicles outside (ρ_location = 0). (2) When multiple vehicles block each other and they are within the intersection, the vehicle having the larger yielding cost has the higher priority. (3) When multiple vehicles block each other and they are outside the intersection, the vehicles use the random numbers to break a tie." Vehicles with ρ_location = 0 in a mixed jam "have to start the backing operation, because they are blocking other vehicles at the intersection."
- Follower-aware yielding cost: χ(v_i) = s·D_SPACE + t·N_f, where D_SPACE = physical distance to a passing place / evacuation site / intersection entrance, and N_f = number of following vehicles (from V2V beacons; a Boolean G from onboard perception in the non-communicating variant). Larger cost ⇒ harder to back up ⇒ higher priority to stay. "When one vehicle blocks on-coming multiple vehicles and leads a deadlock, it is preferable that the single vehicle yields and recedes, because the backing operation of multiple vehicles might be difficult and time-consuming."
- **Sequenced retreat without a coordinator:** each vehicle computes threshold waiting time Δ_i = a·χ(v_i) + R_i (R_i random tie-breaker) and begins backing when its wait exceeds Δ_i — cheapest-to-move robot retreats first, most-expensive last; the ordering emerges from staggered timers rather than negotiation. Explicit rejection of exponential backoff: "A simple exponential backoff used in Ethernet … is neither efficient nor optimal … because the vehicle movements to yield and recede are time-consuming maneuvers."
- Retreat trajectory: "when vehicles conduct a backing operation, they simply go back the way they came, by using the map database and defined path" — retreat along the arrival arm, identical in kind to our yield primitive.
- Results: in AutoSim (SAE J2735 BSM at 10 Hz, 400 m V2V range), across 200–800 vehicles/hour and intersection sizes 10–100 m, A-DRIVE beats a lane-priority baseline on average and worst-case trip delay; notably, under the fixed lane-priority rule "the vehicles on lower-priority lane sometimes have to recede multiple times. In particular, for the long intersection, the recovery times from deadlocks become long time and the trip delays are exponentially increased" — evidence that a *static* priority rule causes repeated, wasted retreats and that cost-aware, situation-dependent selection of the yielder matters.
- Assumptions/limits stated: evacuation/passing spaces are assumed not to be bottlenecks; reasonable traffic volumes; reverse capability along known path assumed.
- **[UN-JAM]** Jäger & Nebel IROS 2001 (see KQ2): resolution order is *successive* — involved robots are asked one at a time for an alternative trajectory "until the deadlock is resolved"; no simultaneous multi-robot retreat — [PDF](https://gki.informatik.uni-freiburg.de/papers/jaeger-nebel-iros01.pdf).
- **[UN-JAM]** Grover, Liu, Sycara (IJRR/arXiv 2022): "After Deadlock" section develops "provably-correct decentralized controllers to make the robots exit the deadlock set," demonstrated on 2–3 robots in simulation and on Khepera-IV hardware; the design explicitly rejects additive random perturbations ("they lack formal guarantees") in favor of controllers derived from the deadlock set's geometry — [arXiv:2206.01781](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.01781).
- **[UN-JAM]** Foundation-model leader election (Garg et al. 2024): the LLM/VLM picks *one* leader robot and routes it via waypoints while the rest hold under a GNN safety controller — an ordering rule of "move exactly one, chosen by semantic reasoning" — [arXiv:2404.06413](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.06413).
- Social mini-games line (doorways/intersections/corridors): symmetric approaches "can converge to undesirable equilibria, including deadlocks or oscillatory behavior" absent "an explicit symmetry-breaking mechanism"; Chandra et al. give (a) a game-theoretic discrete-time-CBF controller that modifies agents' *desired velocities* (not the safety filter) to prevent doorway/intersection deadlock ([arXiv:2308.10966](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10966), 2023), and (b) a bi-level scheme whose "top-level optimization computes a fair and collision-free ordering" of agents through the constrained region, with bottom-level trajectory optimization conditioned on that ordering ([IROS 2023 MAD-Games workshop paper](https://iros2023-madgames.f1tenth.org/papers/chandra.pdf)); taxonomy of these scenarios in [arXiv:2508.13459](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.13459) (2025).
- A-DRIVE's related-work section situates rule-based intersection management: Autonomous Intersection Management reservation protocol (Dresner & Stone, AAMAS 2004/JAIR), arrival-time priority protocols (Azimi et al., ICCPS 2013/2014), synchronous protocols (Ballroom/BRIP, CSIP, DSIP), and Petri-net/graph formal verification (Lin et al., ACM TECS 2019) — and states its distinction: "These studies presented decision-making mechanisms for many different types of driving contexts, but did not consider how multiple vehicles recover from the vehicle deadlock" — [arXiv:2204.04910 §V](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.04910).

### Inferences
- Consensus ordering principles across all four un-jamming sources: (1) minimize the number of retreating robots (prefer one yielder); (2) the yielder is the one with lowest retreat cost — shortest back-out distance, fewest followers stacked behind it, nearest refuge; (3) robots already inside the conflict region finish crossing before outsiders enter (clear the core first); (4) ties must be broken by explicit randomness or external election, because symmetric jams are stable; (5) retreats, when multiple are unavoidable, are sequenced (staggered), never simultaneous.
- For a four-way jam where all four are wedged (every arm blocked behind its robot by followers or all four at the mouth): A-DRIVE's cost logic prescribes backing out the robot with fewest followers/shortest clear reverse path first; after one arm clears, the remaining three resolve by ordinary crossing priority — supporting a composite "one retreats, then ordered crossing" two-stage command rather than multi-robot simultaneous retreat.

### Gaps
- No paper found that analyzes retreat ordering for jams where *every* candidate yielder has followers blocking its reverse path (cascaded multi-robot backup along one arm); A-DRIVE assumes evacuation space exists and explicitly defers "priority assignment that accounts for the space constraints of passing places and evacuation spaces" to future work.
- No formal optimality result found for which-robot-retreats selection (all rules are heuristic costs); no benchmark suite for "formed jam recovery" comparable to the Stern et al. MAPF benchmarks.

## KQ5: Spacing/safety-floor assumptions in the literature vs our hard 0.45 m pairwise floor and per-arm retreat capability

### Takeaway
The literature's spacing assumptions are structural rather than metric: grid MAPF enforces one-robot-per-cell (spacing = cell pitch, collision defined logically); CBF work uses an abstract margin D_s and proves deadlocked robots converge to exactly D_s separation; AGV zone control reserves blocks precisely "to make room between AGVs"; A-DRIVE assumes reverse-along-known-path plus reachable evacuation space. A hard numeric pairwise floor like 0.45 m maps directly onto D_s, and the key literature result about it is negative: at deadlock you are *at* the floor, so the first legal motion is necessarily a retreat.

### Cited Findings
- Grid MAPF: agents occupy exactly one vertex per timestep; safety = absence of vertex/swap conflicts; physical spacing is delegated to cell size — [Stern et al., SoCS 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08291); RHCR uses 4-neighbor grids (33×46 fulfillment map, 37×77 sorting map) with this abstraction — [arXiv:2005.07371](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07371).
- Kinematic realism is bolted on afterwards: MAPF-with-kinematic-constraints post-processing (Hönig et al., ICAPS 2016) and ADG execution guarantee the *ordering* that preserved spacing at plan time (Hönig et al., RA-L 2019) — [RA-L PDF](https://whoenig.github.io/publications/2019_RA-L_Hoenig.pdf).
- CBF safety floor: pairwise safety function h_ij = ‖Δp_ij‖² − D_s² ≥ 0 with margin D_s; Theorem 3: "In deadlock, the two robots are separated by the safety distance and the robots are on the verge of violating safety"; active-constraint neighbors are "exactly D_s away" — [Grover et al., arXiv:2206.01781](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.01781).
- Container-terminal grid-blocks "reserved in advance … to make room between AGVs and to prevent deadlocks" — spacing enforced by advance block reservation, not by reactive floors — [Springer, OR Spectrum 2006](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-49550-5_12).
- A-DRIVE spatial assumptions: yielding cost scales with distance D_SPACE to a passing place/evacuation site; protocol "assume[s] that a long single-track lane has reasonable traffic volumes and that the evacuation spaces are not the bottleneck"; retreat path = the vehicle's own recorded arrival path from the map database — [arXiv:2204.04910](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.04910).
- Jäger & Nebel trigger coordination metrically: robots exchange trajectories "whenever the distance between two robots drops below a certain value" — a communication/coordination radius layered above the collision radius — [IROS 2001 PDF](https://gki.informatik.uni-freiburg.de/papers/jaeger-nebel-iros01.pdf).

### Inferences
- Our 0.45 m pairwise floor is the direct analogue of D_s, and Grover et al.'s result predicts that a genuinely formed jam in our arena will present with pairwise gaps *at or near* 0.45 m (robots pinned on the constraint boundary). Two practical consequences: (a) M1's STALE-vs-STOPPED distinction matters most exactly there (overlapping/near markers — cf. our own project finding); (b) the safety layer must whitelist motion components that *increase* pairwise distance (reverse along arrival arm) even when the floor is active, otherwise the safety filter itself re-creates the CBF stall that Grover et al. analyze.
- Per-arm retreat capability is a *stronger* assumption than most of the literature makes (A-DRIVE has to price retreat feasibility into χ because followers may block reverse). If our scenarios guarantee a clear arrival arm behind each robot, the yielder-selection problem degenerates to cost ranking without feasibility gating — simpler than A-DRIVE's general case.

### Gaps
- No paper found publishing a comparable absolute floor (e.g., 0.45 m) with junction-jam statistics; metric floors appear only as symbolic D_s or vendor safety-field settings (unpublished).
- No literature located on retreat under *partial* reverse feasibility (follower must first back up to free the yielder) — the cascaded two-robot backup case is unstudied as far as this pass found.

## Design implications for a single-yield-command system (one robot backs out along its arrival arm, others cross in order), considering a composite two-stage command for four-way jams

### Takeaway
The literature validates the yield primitive as the canonical recovery action and supplies concrete selection/ordering rules to govern it; for four-way jams it supports a composite command of the form "lowest-retreat-cost robot backs out → remaining robots cross in a fixed order → yielder re-enters last," executed as one atomic recovery episode with staggered (never simultaneous) retreats if more than one backup proves necessary.

### Cited Findings
(Consolidated from sources above; URLs repeated for self-containment.)
- At least one robot must recede — tie-breaking alone is insufficient once robots are wedged: "vehicles have to break a tie and/or at least one vehicle has to yield and recede" — [A-DRIVE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.04910).
- Prefer exactly one yielder; multi-robot backing is "difficult and time- and fuel-consuming" — [A-DRIVE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.04910).
- Yielder selection = min retreat cost: distance to free space + robots stacked behind (follower-aware cost χ) — [A-DRIVE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.04910); "move exactly one, chosen by high-level semantic reasoner" is also the foundation-model recipe — [arXiv:2404.06413](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.06413).
- Crossing order after the yield: robots inside the junction clear first; outsiders enter by priority; static lane-priority rules cause repeated re-yields and exponentially growing delay at large junctions, so priority should be computed per-episode, not fixed per-arm — [A-DRIVE evaluation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.04910).
- Retreat along the arrival path using the map/recorded path is the standard retreat trajectory — [A-DRIVE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.04910); refuge cells ("spare points," "passing places") are pre-designated — [IEEE 7985596](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7985596), [A-DRIVE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.04910).
- Symmetric four-way ties are stable equilibria and need injected asymmetry (randomness, election, or cost asymmetry) — [Grover et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.01781), [IFAC 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405896320334042), [social mini-games](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10966).
- If retreats must be sequenced, use cost-staggered timing, not simultaneous motion and not exponential backoff — [A-DRIVE Δ_i = a·χ + R_i](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.04910).
- Detection side: dwell-time thresholds in WAIT/CROSSING states plus mutual-blocking check is the deployed detection recipe (A-DRIVE's state machine; wait-for-graph cycle detection in resource terms — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wait-for_graph)); RHCR's lesson is that a progress/potential measure (agents making no progress within a window) is a cheap centralized detector — [arXiv:2005.07371 §4.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07371).
- Fairness guard: throughput-greedy junction policies can starve one arm indefinitely (RHCR's own incompleteness example), so a recovery layer should include aging/timeout escalation — [arXiv:2005.07371 §4.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07371).

### Inferences
- The single-yield primitive is not a simplification of the literature — it *is* the literature's primitive (A-DRIVE's YIELDING state; leader-election-with-hold). What the literature adds is the selection rule (min χ: shortest clear reverse path, fewest followers, nearest refuge; our per-arm retreat guarantee makes followers the dominant term) and the anti-oscillation guard (once a yielder is chosen, others must not also retreat — enforce via the staggered-timer idea or explicit M5 lock on the recovery episode).
- For the composite four-way command, the supported design is two-stage-atomic: Stage 1 = single yield by min-cost robot; Stage 2 = ordered crossing of the remaining three (inside-first, then per-episode priority), with the yielder re-entering last. A three- or four-robot simultaneous retreat has no support anywhere in the surveyed literature and is explicitly discouraged (difficult, time-consuming, oscillation-prone). If Stage 1's yielder cannot clear the core (blocked reverse), the fallback the literature suggests is *re-selection* of a different yielder (A-DRIVE re-negotiation) rather than escalating to multi-robot retreat.
- Because a formed jam sits at the 0.45 m floor (Grover et al.), the M4 safety layer must classify "reverse along arrival arm" as distance-increasing and exempt it from the min-distance stop, or the yield command will be vetoed by the very invariant that made the jam stable. (Consistent with our existing backup-phase exemption work.)
- The MAPF/industrial doctrine (reserve-the-junction, one-holder semantics) is worth importing *after* recovery: granting M5 a transient junction lock during the crossing stage reproduces the zone-control invariant that prevents immediate re-jamming.

### Gaps
- No source provides a verified procedure for the worst case "all four arms blocked behind all four robots" (no feasible single retreat); this remains a genuine open design question — closest guidance is A-DRIVE's future-work note on space-constrained evacuation and the re-selection loop.
- No quantitative guidance found on how long to wait before declaring deadlock (thresholds are tuned scale factors in A-DRIVE; RHCR's p is user-specified); needs empirical tuning in our arena.
